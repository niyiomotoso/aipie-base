## Recording copy

There's something I find really interesting about all the conversations we're having today about people getting emotionally attached to AI. Because we were already seeing a version of this almost sixty years ago.

Back in the mid-1960s, a computer scientist at MIT named Joseph Weizenbaum built a program called ELIZA. And when I say chatbot, please don't imagine anything remotely close to ChatGPT. You'd type a message on a machine that printed its replies on paper, and ELIZA would look for certain words and use prepared rules to respond.

One of its scripts, called DOCTOR, acted a bit like a therapist. Not by actually understanding your problems, but by turning what you said into another question. So the more you talked, the more it could seem like somebody was listening.

Now, here's where the story gets interesting.

Weizenbaum later wrote about his secretary trying ELIZA. She knew he'd been working on the program for months, but after exchanging a few messages with it, she asked him to leave the room. She wanted to continue the conversation privately.

I mean, imagine that. You know it's software, and yet you start treating it like somebody you can confide in.

And Weizenbaum wasn't delighted by that reaction. He became disturbed by how readily people could mistake a convincing response for genuine understanding. He later wrote extensively about the limits of computer power and why human judgement still matters.

What gets me is that today's AI is vastly more capable than ELIZA. But that human instinct to hear a convincing reply and think, 'this thing understands me'? We've been grappling with that for a very long time.
