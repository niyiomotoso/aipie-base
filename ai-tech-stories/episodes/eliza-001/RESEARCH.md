# ELIZA: Research dossier (working draft)

**Status:** Research candidate. Script and visual concepts not yet approved.  
**Proposed title:** The man who built a chatbot and became unsettled by people's reactions  
**Central question:** Why did the reaction to an early conversational program trouble its creator?  
**Primary human character:** Joseph Weizenbaum, an MIT computer scientist.  
**Setting:** MIT, 1960s; later reaction and critique through 1976.

## Narrative promise

Today's debates about confiding in AI have a striking historical precedent. In the mid-1960s, Weizenbaum developed ELIZA, which simulated a conversation using rules and pattern matching. People sometimes treated it as though it understood them. He was disturbed less by a mysterious breakthrough in computer intelligence than by what human users projected onto the machine.

### Story angles considered

1. **Preferred: The invention that unsettled its inventor.** Follow Weizenbaum's surprise and philosophical shift. Strong character-driven structure and modern relevance.
2. The apparent therapist that didn't understand a word. Strong demonstration, but less human drama.
3. The chatbot whose lost code was rediscovered in 2021. Excellent secondary story, but weaker emotional link for our first pilot.

**Why angle 1:** A human with a clear motivation and a surprising reaction. The 'secretary asked for privacy' moment is a memorable mid-story pivot, but is presented only as Weizenbaum's own recollection, not an independently verified event.

## Verified historical timeline

- **1964–1965:** Weizenbaum builds and evolves ELIZA at MIT; a 1965 source-code printout is now held in MIT's archives. The program was developed over multiple versions rather than appearing fully formed in 1966. Sources S1, S2, S3.
- **January 1966:** His paper about ELIZA appears in *Communications of the ACM*, volume 9, pages 36–45. It explains keyword handling and transformation rules, including the DOCTOR script. Sources S1, S2.
- **1960s:** The DOCTOR script emulates an open-ended psychotherapist by redirecting users' statements into questions. It does not have human understanding. Sources S1, S2.
- **1976:** Weizenbaum publishes *Computer Power and Human Reason*. He describes his surprise at people's reactions to ELIZA and argues that some decisions should not be delegated to computers simply because they might be technically possible. Sources S4, S5.
- **2021 onward:** Researchers recover historical ELIZA code from the MIT archive and study its versions, published examples and reception. Important for verification and future episodes. Sources S2, S3, S6, S7.

## Editorial red lines

- Avoid saying ELIZA 'understood', 'thought', 'learned like ChatGPT' or provided actual therapy.
- Avoid saying users widely 'fell in love with' ELIZA. Weizenbaum documented his perception of anthropomorphism and intimate disclosure, not a population-wide measure of romantic bonds.
- Never claim the private-conversation anecdote has been independently verified. Recent archival research explicitly highlights uncertainties about the story.
- Do not attribute words from published demonstration dialogues to a verified real, unedited user session.
- Avoid 'invented in 1966' if speaking precisely: it was published in 1966 and developed earlier.
- The contemporary parallel is a thoughtful observation, not evidence that today's LLMs function the same way as ELIZA.
- Do not turn Weizenbaum into a simplistic anti-technology prophet. His critique included how people attribute authority to computers and what decisions humans ought to retain.
