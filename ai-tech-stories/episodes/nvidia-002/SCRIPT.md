## Recording copy

You know what I find fascinating about NVIDIA? It's not just how big the company has become because of AI. It's that technology people associated with video games helped change the whole industry.

Back in 2012, a researcher named Alex Krizhevsky was training a computer to recognise what was in photographs. That is, imagine showing a computer a photo of a dog, and it tells you, "That's a dog", without you typing the answer.

And where was he doing this? On a computer in his bedroom at his parents' house, using two NVIDIA graphics cards. Yes, the kind of hardware people bought to make video games look better.

But here's why that matters.

NVIDIA started out in the 1990s making graphics technology. And graphics cards are brilliant at handling lots of calculations at the same time. Imagine having many people work on different pieces of one big puzzle, instead of one person doing everything.

Then in 2006, NVIDIA introduced something called CUDA. In plain English, it made it easier for developers to use that computing power for things beyond games, including scientific research.

Fast forward to 2012. Krizhevsky, alongside Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton, developed a system called AlexNet. They entered a competition where computers had to identify what was in pictures, and their system performed far better than the competition.

That became one of the defining moments for modern AI. It showed just how far computers could go when you trained them using enormous collections of examples.

And what gets me is that CUDA wasn't built for the ChatGPT-style AI market we know today. NVIDIA made those chips useful for more problems, and years later, that helped researchers achieve something extraordinary.

So I'm curious. If you were running NVIDIA back in 2006, would you have made that investment without knowing just how big the opportunity might become?
