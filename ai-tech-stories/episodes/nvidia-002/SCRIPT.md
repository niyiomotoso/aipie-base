## Recording copy

You know what I find fascinating about NVIDIA? It's not even how big the company has become because of AI. It's how something people mostly associated with playing video games ended up helping change the whole industry.

Let me explain.

Back in 2012, a researcher named Alex Krizhevsky was working on a computer system that could recognise what was in photographs. That is, imagine showing a computer a picture of a dog, and without you typing the answer, it can tell you, "That's a dog."

Now, what's interesting is where he was training this thing. He had a computer in his bedroom at his parents' house, using just two NVIDIA graphics cards. Yes, the kind of hardware people were buying to make games run better.

But to understand why that mattered, we need to go back a few years.

NVIDIA started out in the 1990s building graphics technology for games and multimedia. And it turns out that graphics cards are very good at doing lots of calculations at the same time. Think of it like having hundreds of people working on different pieces of a huge puzzle at once, rather than one person doing every piece alone.

So in 2006, NVIDIA introduced something called CUDA. In plain English, it gave developers a much easier way to use that computing power for jobs other than graphics, including scientific research.

Fast forward to 2012. Krizhevsky, working with Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton, developed a system called AlexNet. They entered it into a major competition where computers had to identify what was in photographs, and it performed far better than the competing approaches.

That became one of the defining moments of modern deep learning.

And what gets me is that CUDA wasn't built for the ChatGPT-style AI market we know today. NVIDIA made its chips more useful to people working on entirely different problems, and years later, that helped enable a breakthrough that changed the field.

So here's what I'm curious about. If you were running NVIDIA back in 2006, would you have invested in making gaming chips useful for all these other things, without knowing how big the opportunity might become?
