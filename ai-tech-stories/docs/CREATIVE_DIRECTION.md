# Creative direction: initial working hypotheses

This is not a style guide copied from any previous AI Pie content. We will discover the new format through prototypes and Niyi's feedback. Nothing here is frozen.

## Intended experience

A fascinating story told by a real person who is genuinely interested in what happened. Entertaining and informed, a little cinematic but not theatrical. Conversational, personal and easy to follow even for non-engineers.

## Editorial voice

- Use 'I', 'me', personal observations and reactions when they genuinely add meaning.
- Avoid third-person textbook scripts, fake autobiographical experiences and exaggerated dialogue.
- Do not reduce narration to fragments or formulaic dramatic one-liners.
- No obligation to end with a lesson, a question or a CTA. But where the story genuinely invites an opinion, dilemma or personal reflection, include an optional conversational audience question at the beginning, middle or end. Never force generic engagement bait.
- The audience should feel it has heard a great story, not been lectured.
- Authentic technical perspective is welcome, but avoid jargon-heavy explanations.
- **Plain English is central to the series promise.** Every key idea must be understandable and imaginable by someone without a technology background. When a phrase is technically accurate but abstract (e.g. 'recognise objects in photographs'), bridge it to a simple, concrete picture ('That is, imagine showing a computer a photo of a dog, and it tells you it's a dog').
- Explain unfamiliar terms or mechanisms only when they matter. Use brief everyday analogies, tangible examples and conversational transitions such as 'that is', 'in other words' or 'imagine', rather than textbook definitions. Do not make explanations repetitive, condescending or technically misleading.

## Subjects

People, companies, countries, turning points, strange historical projects, rivalries, overlooked inventions, failures, mistakes, and surprising consequences. Mix familiar names with obscure but compelling events. Stories may be historical or contemporary, provided the background and evidence support the narrative.

## Story anatomy (flexible)

1. Establish a truthful curiosity gap immediately.
2. Set the scene with a person, decision, risk or historical context.
3. Escalate tension with the unusual discovery or turning point.
4. Pay off the opening and explain what happened.
5. Offer a genuine first-person reflection if it earns its place.
6. Consider a genuinely useful audience question if it arises naturally from the story; no forced ending CTA.

Target 75-120 seconds as an experiment, not a strict limit. Each video should work without watching another episode first.

## Visual language

Balanced hybrid: try 40% on-camera narration and 60% archival / graphical material initially. Return to the host at interpretive turns, emotional beats and conclusions. Visuals should advance the story, not simply fill time. Prefer restrained typography and motion graphics over distracting effects.

## Story gate

Score each possible story from 0-5 for curiosity, tension, human stakes, relevance and source quality. Research before locking the angle. More than one strong source is preferable for contentious claims. Retain claim-to-source mapping.

## Decisions not yet locked

- Series name and packaging
- Exact on-camera/visual balance
- Typical video duration
- Music and graphics identity
- First five test episodes
- Degree of humour, personal reflection and suspense

Do not automate these creative decisions prematurely. Gather examples, prototype and revise based on approved episodes.
